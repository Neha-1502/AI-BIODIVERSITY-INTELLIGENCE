SOURCE: Summary notes on Soil Organic Carbon research (based on FAO Global Soil Partnership publications)
TOPIC: soil_organic_carbon, cover_crops, microbial_diversity

Soil organic carbon (SOC) is widely used as a core indicator of soil health because it
directly influences nutrient cycling, water retention, and microbial habitat availability.
Degraded agricultural soils, particularly under continuous monoculture, commonly show SOC
levels below 0.5%, which limits the soil's capacity to support diverse microbial communities.

FAO-aligned conservation agriculture research indicates that introducing leguminous cover
crops (such as clover or vetch) into rotation cycles adds nitrogen-rich organic matter to
the soil. Multi-year field trials referenced in FAO conservation agriculture literature
report SOC increases in the range of roughly 15-25% over a 2-3 year period following
sustained cover crop adoption, compared to continuous monoculture baselines.

Increases in SOC are associated with higher microbial biomass and functional diversity,
because organic carbon serves as the primary energy substrate for soil microorganisms.
Greater microbial diversity in turn supports healthier root-associated fungal networks
(mycorrhizae), which improve plant nutrient uptake and indirectly support flowering plant
vigor -- a factor relevant to pollinator food availability.

Reduced or no-till practices, when combined with cover cropping, further preserve soil
aggregate structure and prevent the disruption of microbial habitat that conventional
tillage causes. This combination is generally recommended in semi-arid and degraded soil
contexts where organic matter is already scarce.
