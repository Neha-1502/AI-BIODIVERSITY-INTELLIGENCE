SOURCE: Summary notes on Water Availability and Biodiversity Survival (based on IPCC AR6
and IPBES Global Assessment Report themes)
TOPIC: rainfall, water_availability, species_survival, pollution

In semi-arid and arid regions (annual rainfall below roughly 600mm and 300mm respectively),
water availability is frequently the primary limiting factor for both crop viability and
wild species survival. Reduced or erratic rainfall lowers soil moisture below the threshold
many amphibian, invertebrate, and understory plant species require to complete life cycles.

IPBES assessments identify water stress as a compounding factor alongside land degradation:
soils with low organic carbon have reduced water-holding capacity, meaning the same rainfall
deficit produces more severe moisture stress on degraded land than on organically rich soil.
This creates a feedback loop where soil degradation and water scarcity reinforce each other.

Mulching and reduced tillage are commonly recommended low-cost interventions for
water-stressed agricultural land, since surface mulch reduces direct evaporation and
moderates soil temperature. Field-level studies referenced in conservation agriculture
literature report soil moisture retention improvements in the range of 10-20% during dry
periods when mulching is combined with reduced tillage.

Separately, agricultural runoff containing nitrates and pesticides is identified as a major
driver of aquatic biodiversity loss, independent of rainfall volume. Riparian buffer strips
and vegetated margins near water bodies are associated with meaningful reductions in
nutrient runoff reaching waterways, which supports recovery of aquatic invertebrate and
amphibian populations over time.
